    How to read this page

    What it measures

    For any group of census tracts, we estimate what residents spend in each category, using national spending patterns for households at their incomes, and what stores located there sell. Both come from public data: Census and BLS surveys of households, and Census counts of stores and jobs. It is not card data, so it shows where spending likely goes, not where residents actually shop.

    How to read it

    The retention rate is local store sales divided by what the national pattern predicts from residents' spending. 1.0 is the national norm, an average over every place in the country. Downtowns, malls and corridors that draw shoppers from elsewhere pull it up, so residential neighborhoods usually fall below it, while regional towns and small cities that serve the countryside around them often sit above it. The page therefore compares each area first with similar places (the same kind of area, setting and size across the US). A category in the bottom quarter of similar places is where residents' spending goes elsewhere; in the top quarter, the area draws shoppers in. More leaves than in the typical one is the dollars of residents' spending the typical similar place keeps but this area doesn't; leakage at the national norm is the same measure against the national average, shown as a second yardstick. Figures are ranges because store sales are estimated two ways, and real uncertainty is wider. Store counts say whether the gap is room for more stores or stores already here that are short of customers. Treat all of it as a planning estimate, not a sales forecast. Full method and limits

    Stores: room for more, or spending elsewhere

    How each reading is decided

    "Carried at the national norm" is how many stores of the kind residents' spending in that category would carry at the US ratio of stores to spending. That norm is an average over every place in the country, so it sits above what most residential neighborhoods keep and below what many regional towns keep; "carried at the rate similar places keep" scales it by the median retention of places of the same kind, setting and size, and "room for more" is that figure minus the stores here. Sales per store compares sales per store here with the US average: well under 1.00 means the stores already here are short of customers, so residents' spending goes elsewhere even though the stores exist. Each ratio uses the same listing on both sides, so a listing that runs high everywhere cancels out. Map listings sort restaurants by cuisine, not service, so each tract's restaurants are split into full and quick service by the mix of restaurants in its ZIP codes (ZIP Business Patterns 2023), and the US counts the same way. Coffee shops, bakeries and dessert shops (cafés, juice and bubble tea, ice cream, candy) are a separate row; convenience stores and bodegas are counted with the food stores that take SNAP, under Stores here. These are planning estimates from public data, not a market study.

    Compared with similar places

    Similar places are areas of the same kind (city or town, county, or neighborhood), the same setting and a similar population, measured the same way across the US. Typical is their median retention rate, at the middle of the two estimates; the middle half runs from the 25th to the 75th percentile. Retention varies widely even among similar places, so read this as a reference, not a standard.

    Compared with nearby areas, and side by side

    Side by side with another area

    Every area is measured the same way as the selection above and compared with places of its own kind, setting and size, so a large neighborhood and a small one are each judged against their own kind. "Here ÷ typical" is the area's retention over the typical retention of its similar places (each category's typical rate weighted by its expected sales); 1.0 means it keeps as much as they do. Similar places are grouped by kind, setting and population band, so the typical value can step at a band's edge, and two areas just either side of one are judged against different groups. Click an area to open it; the view and any second area stay with it.

    Where the spending goes nearby

    Projected leakage of the area within 10, 25 and 50 miles of the selection (straight line from the nearest selected tract, the selection itself left out), at the middle of the two estimates; a surplus means its stores sell more than its residents' spending supports. "Balanced by" is the nearest ring whose leakage (or surplus) is at least as large as this area's surplus (or leakage). It shows where offsetting spending could come from or go, not where any one shopper goes, and it leaves out online spending and travel.

      Method and limits

      What this measures

      This is a retail-gap estimate, not observed leakage. Local sales include shoppers who live elsewhere, and resident demand is modeled from national spending patterns by income. Neither side records where residents actually shop. That takes card data split by cardholder home and store location, which is the next layer (see the bottom of this page).

      Census tracts

      Census tracts are small areas the Census Bureau draws to hold about 4,000 people each (1,200 to 8,000). Every figure here is added up from tracts, so a named neighborhood, city or ZIP code is approximated by the tracts that lie mostly inside it; the fit note under each area says how closely.

      Household income

      Total household income is the ACS aggregate household income (B19025), before taxes, in 2024 dollars; where the Census does not publish a tract's total, it is estimated from the households in each income bracket, and the page says so. The median is estimated from the households in each income bracket (B19001), within the bracket that holds the middle household ("$200,000 or more" when it falls in the top bracket); the Census's own tract medians cannot be added up across tracts. The income chart compares the share of households in each bracket with the US.

      Resident demand

      Residents are each tract's total population and households its occupied housing units (ACS 2020–2024 five-year, tables B01003 and B19001). Residents include people living in group quarters, such as dorms, prisons and nursing homes, who are not households and are not counted in demand; a tract that is mostly group quarters can show many residents and little or no demand. Demand is households by income bracket in each tract (B19001) times what US households at each income spend by category (BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey 2024), interpolated at each bracket's midpoint. The top bracket's mean is solved from aggregate household income (B19025), held between $200K and $1M. The total household income in the sentence at the top is that same aggregate (2024 dollars, before taxes; for the few tracts where the Census does not publish it, it is estimated from the income brackets): what residents earn, not what they spend, given only for scale, along with the share of it the measured categories take. Spending here is the model's estimate from the survey, which runs below actual spending, so the share leans low; where many households report little income (students, people living on savings, aid or benefits not counted as income) it can approach or pass all of reported income, as the survey itself shows at low incomes. Gasoline uses 2022 survey spending at 2022 incomes, restated at 2024 pump prices. Gas, auto repair and vehicle purchases are scaled by the tract's vehicles per household (B25046) against what the survey shows at the same incomes, between 0.2 and 2.5 times.

      Rural households spend differently from what their incomes alone predict. The survey's urban and rural tables (2015–2019, the latest by area on FRED) show them spending less on restaurants, alcohol, personal care and apparel, and more on tobacco, after income is accounted for two ways (the tracts' own incomes, and the survey's rural and urban mean incomes). Each tract's demand in those categories is multiplied by 1 − r + r × f, where r is its rural share (the part of its 2020 population outside Census urban areas) and f is the category's factor: restaurants 0.83, alcohol 0.61, personal care 0.73, apparel 0.75 and tobacco 1.73. A fully rural tract takes the whole factor; a fully urban one is unchanged. Groceries are not adjusted: rural households spend on food at home about what their incomes predict, and the survey leaves out food people grow themselves. A factor is used only where both ways put it at least 0.1 away from 1 on the same side and rural households spend less (or more) than urban ones in every one of the five years; the other categories are left unadjusted.

      Categories

      Fourteen categories are measured on both sides: groceries; full-service restaurants; quick-service restaurants, coffee and food trucks; alcohol, from stores and bars; tobacco and vaping; household supplies, garden and stationery; pets, toys, hobbies and books; personal care products and services; apparel, shoes and clothing care; furniture, home furnishings, appliances and computers; gasoline; auto parts and repair; fitness, sports and recreation (gyms and studios, golf, bowling, skiing, and sports, dance and music lessons); and child care (day care centers, preschools and babysitting). Medicines and other fees and admissions (movies, shows, events) are demand only.

      Stores sell across categories, so local sales are counted by what is sold, not by store type. Each store type's sales are split by what that kind of store sells nationally, from the 2022 Economic Census product lines: a supermarket's sales go mostly to groceries, partly to household supplies, personal care and alcohol, and its pharmacy counter to no category; a gas station's go to gasoline, groceries, tobacco and alcohol. Medicines and vitamins, prescription or over the counter, fall in no category, matching the demand-only medicines line; so do lines no category covers, such as sporting goods and used goods.

      Full-service and quick-service restaurants split cleanly on the sales side. On the spending side, each tract's restaurant spending is split by the share that households at each income spend at full-service restaurants, from the BLS Consumer Expenditure Diary Survey 2024 public-use files: about 40%–45% for households under $100,000, 46%–48% from $100,000 to $200,000 and 52% above $200,000 (46% for all households; each income range's share has a standard error of 2–5 points). Food from vending machines and mobile vendors counts as quick-service. Employer and school cafeterias and catered events are left out when the share is measured, but their spending stays in restaurant demand and is split in the same proportion.

      Local sales: two estimates

      • Jobs-based (LEHD LODES 2023): retail, food-service and personal-service jobs on each block, split into store types by the ZIP's industry mix and aligned with Economic Census employment for the state.
      • ZIP-based (ZIP Business Patterns 2023): each ZIP's employment by store type, with each Census-suppressed size class given the fewest stores the suppression rule allows, each store counted at its size class's midpoint, and the remainder scaled to the ZIP's published sector total, then allocated to tracts by their share of the ZIP's jobs in that sector. ZIP Business Patterns leaves a kind of business unnamed where a ZIP has only one or two of them, and both estimates split a ZIP's jobs by the kinds it names, so unnamed stores are counted as the named kinds. Where some of a ZIP's businesses in a sector are unnamed (outside the named kinds, or inside a named kind beyond its named sub-kinds) and none of the named ones is a kind this site counts, the unnamed businesses are split, on both estimates, by the kinds of business Census leaves unnamed in the state's counties of the same kind, metropolitan, small-city or rural (County Business Patterns 2023 counts each county's businesses by kind wherever a county has three or more of a kind, and the state's count supplies the rest; what each county's ZIP codes name is taken away), each at its kind's typical size there (the middle of its size classes, or, for a kind too rare there to have one, such as department stores in rural counties, the middle over all counties), within the sector jobs the named kinds leave. A ZIP with only one or two businesses in a sector has no row for it at all, and some ZIP codes with jobs aren't listed at all (campuses, public or tribal employers, or businesses counted under a neighboring ZIP code); the ZIP-based estimate counts none there. Where any of this covers a quarter or more of an area's retail, service or restaurant jobs, the page says so. This estimate puts more jobs in gas stations with convenience stores than County Business Patterns' state employment does (up to 1.59 times, in Massachusetts) and fewer in other gas stations (below it in 47 states and DC); across all gas stations it runs from 0.88 to 1.57 times County Business Patterns (California 1.26, Illinois 1.20), so gas sales on the ZIP-based estimate may be over- or understated, and are most likely overstated in New England (Massachusetts, Connecticut, New Hampshire); that is under review.
      • Fitness and child care: gyms and studios, golf courses, bowling centers, ski areas and other recreation, sports, dance and music instruction, and day care centers. Workplace job counts for these industries are mostly public schools, hospitals, casinos and museums, so both estimates use each ZIP's own ZIP Business Patterns rows for them (scaled so their US employment matches the 2022 Economic Census), placed in the ZIP's tracts by their share of its gyms and studios or child care centers listed on Overture Maps (or of its residents where it lists none); each ZIP's marinas are taken out of recreation. Their demand comes from the survey's detailed items by income: club memberships, fees for participant sports and recreational lessons; and babysitting, child care, day care and preschool. Self-employed child care (mostly home day care) and recreation are added by county, placed the same way.
      • Both estimates add self-employed businesses (Census Nonemployer Statistics 2024) for restaurants, bars, salons, barbers and other personal care, auto repair, coin laundries and dry cleaners: each county's receipts, placed in tracts by their share of the county's jobs in that industry. Self-employed retail sellers are left out, because most of their sales are online or direct, and so are food trucks, which the Census reports together with caterers.

      Each store type's jobs are multiplied by its 2022 Economic Census sales per employee, taken from the place, county, metro area, state or US, whichever is the smallest with enough stores (pharmacies always at the US rate), split into categories as above, and restated in 2024 dollars (gasoline at 2024 pump prices). Thirty-four store types are used, from supermarkets and convenience stores to salons, dry cleaners, food trucks, gyms and day care centers; building-material stores, car dealers and online sellers are left out. The two estimates set every range on this page, except fitness and child care, where both use the same ZIP employment and give one figure. Alaska uses LODES 2016 and Michigan 2021, the latest each state has published.

      Retention rate

      Across the US, store sales run 0.9 to 3.5 times modeled resident spending, depending on the category. Most run above it, because households under-report spending in the survey and stores also sell to businesses, commuters and visitors. Pets, toys and books and home furnishings run near or below it, because much of that spending goes online or to store types left out here. The retention rate divides local sales by resident demand times that national ratio, separately for each estimate, so 1.0 means an area sells what the national pattern predicts for its residents. Projected leakage is resident demand times (1 − retention rate): residents' spending, in survey dollars, that local stores would keep at the national norm but do not. Because the survey under-reports some spending, it leans conservative. Store counts instead use the gap in store-sales terms (the predicted sales minus local sales), divided by a benchmark store's sales in that category.

      Check against the South Shore baseline

      For Chicago's South Shore (the City's 15 tracts), this page finds 25,043 households (CMAP's figure is 26,470) and $330M of resident demand in the five categories the hand-built South Shore baseline measured (the baseline has $352M from the same survey items; the difference comes from the household count, the baseline's assumption for top-bracket income, and its single car-ownership adjustment for the whole area where this page adjusts tract by tract). The baseline compared local sales with demand directly, found local sales at 63%–75% of demand and a gap of $88M–$129M. This page measures 13 categories in its total against the national norm and splits store sales by what each kind of store sells, so its figures are larger and not directly comparable: a net projected leakage of $242M–$272M, with retention rates of 0.50–0.57 for groceries, 0.05–0.12 for full-service dining, 0.21–0.44 for quick-service food and 0.12–0.14 for home furnishings. The two agree on where the biggest gaps are: restaurants and everyday goods (here groceries, household supplies, and pets, toys and books). Home furnishings, which the baseline could not measure, shows almost no local sales.

      Check against states' sales-tax records

      Many states publish taxable sales by city or county and by kind of business. Where they do, the estimates here are compared with them.

      Restaurants and bars are the cleanest comparison, since every state taxes restaurant meals. Store goods compare less directly: taxable sales leave out groceries in most states and include goods outside the categories here (electronics, sporting goods, jewelry and more), so the estimates usually come to less than taxable sales there, and the correlation is the test. The largest misses are small towns where tract lines cut through the commercial district, warehouse towns, and places whose sales come mostly from hotels and conventions, like Rosemont, Illinois.

      State by state: sales-tax rates and checks

      Similar places, the surrounding region and visitors

      • Similar places: every Census place (tracts at least half in it), county and named neighborhood in the US with at least 300 households is grouped by kind, setting and population; neighborhoods are filtered as the search filters them, and neighborhoods that cover the same tracts count once. Setting is the county's: in a metropolitan or micropolitan area, or neither (OMB delineation, July 2023); a neighborhood's setting is whether the city holding most of its households has 250,000 people or more. Groups with fewer than 30 areas are merged with a neighboring population band. Small towns count only when a tract lies at least half inside them, so the groups for towns under 2,500 people are small (about 100–550 places) and lean toward towns that fill a whole tract. A ZIP code or hand-picked selection is compared as a neighborhood when most of its households live in such a city, or in a smaller city with at least twice its residents (then with neighborhoods of smaller cities), otherwise as a city or town; a suggested area is compared as a neighborhood. The page leads with this comparison: a category's gap against similar places is resident spending × (their median retention − retention here), each counted as at most 1.0: a rate above 1.0 means a place also draws in shoppers from elsewhere, which is not its residents' spending, so the gap compares only how much of residents' own spending stays and never exceeds what they spend; and its rank among them (bottom quarter, middle half, top quarter) decides whether spending reads as going elsewhere or drawing shoppers in. The national norm stays beside it as a second yardstick: higher than the typical one for most neighborhoods, lower for many regional towns and small cities. A total against similar places adds up each category's own gap; it is not the total of any one typical place.
      • The surrounding region sums the projected leakage of every tract whose interior point lies within 10, 25 or 50 miles of a selected tract's, including neighboring states.
      • Visitor economy: an area is flagged when at least 50 of its homes are seasonal or vacation homes and they are at least 3 times the national share of housing (ACS 2020–2024, B25004 and B25001), or when it has at least 5 hotels, motels and other lodging places and as many per resident as the top 5% of US places with 1,000 or more residents (Overture Maps, October 2026).

      What this site keeps

      The first download in a browser asks for an email address; role, organization and an updates opt-in are optional. They go to the site's spreadsheet, with what was downloaded and the area; only the site's owner and the people they ask to review suggested areas can open it, and the first download from an address is also emailed to the owner. This browser remembers the address. Messages and suggested areas go to the same spreadsheet. The site also counts which areas are looked at and downloaded (the date, state, area name and number of tracts), with no address or identifier, though a counted download can be matched to its download record by date and area. Updates go only to addresses that have confirmed they want them. Page visits are counted by Cloudflare Web Analytics, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data. Nothing is sold or shared.

      Jobs, stores and programs

      • Jobs located here come from the same LEHD LODES files, by workplace and all 20 industry sectors. Jobs held by residents come from LODES residence files, wherever those jobs are. Both count jobs, not people, so their difference is net commuting. Self-employed people are not in either.
      • Hospitals are every hospital on Medicare's list (CMS Hospital General Information, October 2026), placed by the Census Geocoder or, where it can't place the address, by matching the name to a hospital listing on Overture Maps in the same ZIP code (or the same county, where the ZIP is a post-office box or single-site ZIP with no area of its own); about 5% can't be placed either way and are left out. Map listings alone count a hospital's departments and offices as separate hospitals. Community health centers are HRSA's active, permanent health center sites, including school-based ones (not mobile vans or seasonal sites). Doctors' offices and clinics, dentists, urgent care and mental health clinics are Overture Maps listings, private and public alike.
      • Stores here: food stores are USDA's list of every store authorized to take SNAP (downloaded October 2026). Other kinds are places listed in Overture Maps (October 2026 release, places with a confidence score of at least 0.9 and not marked closed), an open dataset compiled from several map sources; it lists more salons, bars and home furnishings stores than Census business counts, so treat those rows as indications. National rates per 10,000 residents use the same sources. The store section splits each tract's listed restaurants into full and quick service by the mix of employer restaurants in its ZIP codes (ZIP Business Patterns 2023), since the listings sort restaurants by cuisine, and the US counts the same way; it compares gyms and studios by count only, since golf, bowling, skiing and lessons are not listed.
      • Sales tax on spending that leaves leaves out fitness and child care, which states tax very differently (gym memberships in some, lessons in fewer, child care almost nowhere) and which the rate tables here do not cover yet. It applies each state's combined state and local sales-tax rates, category by category, to the spending that leaves beyond what the typical similar place loses (only in the categories that fall short of it) and, beside that, to projected leakage at the national norm: the rate where the selected tracts are (the city's when at least half a tract is in it, otherwise the town's or the county's), and only on what each state taxes. Groceries, gas, salon services and repair labor are exempt or taxed differently in many states; personal care, apparel and auto are split between goods and services by the services' national share of sales. Rates come from each state's revenue department (or the Streamlined Sales Tax files its members publish), as of October 2026. Local restaurant taxes are included where a state publishes them (Massachusetts, Mississippi, Montana, and meals taxes in DC, Connecticut, Maine, New Hampshire, Rhode Island and Vermont) and, in Arkansas, the city advertising and promotion ("hamburger") taxes of the 12 cities that publish their rate, and in Florida, the 2% resort tax on restaurant food and drink in Miami Beach, Bal Harbour and Surfside; other local meals taxes, excise taxes and caps on single purchases are not. Where a state's local rates differ by kind of purchase, as New York's do for clothing, New York City's personal services and motor fuel, each kind takes its own rate. Gaps for each state are listed with the figures and in the table below.
      • Programs and designations come from the agencies' tract lists: Treasury's 2018 Opportunity Zones and its September 2026 list of tracts eligible for 2027 zones, with the tracts governors have nominated where a state has published its final list (11 states as of October 27, 2026, named in the table; elsewhere the page says the list is not published; Treasury designates the zones, so a nomination is not a designation); the CDFI Fund's New Markets Tax Credit and CDFI Investment Area eligibility (2016–2020 ACS); USDA's food access maps (2019 supermarket access; 2025 access to any SNAP store); and HUD's Qualified Census Tracts and Difficult Development Areas for the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (2026 and 2027 lists). Difficult Development Areas are drawn by ZIP area in metro areas and by county elsewhere, so a tract counts when at least half its land lies in one (a ZIP area split between HUD metro areas counts only when every part of it is designated). Lists drawn on 2010 tracts are carried to a 2020 tract when at least half its land lies in listed 2010 tracts. "Severely distressed" here applies the three main NMTC tests only; a tract can also qualify in other ways. HUBZones come from SBA's HUBZone map service (its current Qualified Census Tracts and Non-Metropolitan Counties, governor-designated and disaster areas, and Indian lands, a tract counting for Indian lands when at least half of its area is inside).
      • State and local programs (enterprise zones and other state incentive areas, tax increment financing districts, special service areas and business improvement districts, brownfield areas, state designations such as Main Street, cultural and downtown programs, transit-oriented designations, and designated sites) come from the official boundary layers that states, counties, cities and regional agencies publish, read when the site is built. Each district's share of a selection is the part of the selected tracts' land inside it, measured on the generalized 2024 tract shapes in an equal-area projection (shares that round to 1% of a tract or more); a program's total counts land in two overlapping districts once, and districts past their last year in force are left out. Designated sites (points) are listed by the tract they sit in. Coverage is growing place by place, and the programs section names the sources covered in the selected state, so a missing district elsewhere does not mean there is none. South Carolina, Mississippi, Arkansas, Tennessee, North Carolina and Georgia rank every county into tiers for job tax credits and similar incentives, and Alabama's targeted and jumpstart counties are Enterprise Zones county-wide; in those states every county a selection touches is listed with its tier or designation from the state's current list, with its share of the selection's households.
      • Public and land-bank property counts the parcels that 49 large cities publish in their official GIS layers as held by a land bank or land reutilization authority, owned by the city, county or another public agency (surplus land included), or vacant (citywide vacant-lot or vacant-building layers). Where an owner field mixes public agencies and private owners, only public and land-bank parcels are counted, and no owner names are kept. A parcel listed twice counts once, in the tract that holds it. Each city's layer is taken as it stands, so what is counted differs by city and a tract outside these cities has no count; a city list from before 2024 is counted with its year shown, and the page names the layers each area's counts come from. Charlotte's layer is linked rather than counted, because its terms limit reuse.
      • Near transit is measured here, not taken from an agency list: the share of the selection's land within a half mile (804.67 m) of a station, using the USDOT National Transit Map (agencies' GTFS schedules) for subway, light rail, streetcar, commuter rail and monorail stations, and the FTA National Transit Database for bus rapid transit stations and for rail stations of agencies whose schedules the map lacks. Platforms and entrances of one station count once. Cable cars, aerial trams, funiculars and the Alaska Railroad are left out. Bus rapid transit is counted only where the agency reports the line as bus rapid transit, so some rapid bus lines reported as ordinary bus are missing. A half mile is the common station-area distance (FTA, 76 FR 52046).

      Neighborhoods and other areas

      • Neighborhoods outside Chicago come from Who's On First, an open gazetteer. In some cities its boundaries come from the city's own planning or GIS office (Atlanta, Baltimore, Boston, Denver, Los Angeles, New Orleans, Oakland, Portland, San Diego, San Francisco, Seattle and others) or from published maps (Philadelphia, Washington, New York); elsewhere most come from Zetashapes and Quattroshapes, algorithmic boundaries assembled from crowd-sourced place data. Each result names its source. There is no official national neighborhood map, so coverage and quality are uneven. Algorithmic boundaries (Zetashapes, Quattroshapes) that tracts trace poorly (fit under 30%) are left out as noise. Boundaries from every other source are kept at any fit, with a warning when the tracts trace them poorly. Where one city has two overlapping boundaries with the same name, the City's or a city office's own boundary is kept, then the one the tracts trace better; same-name places that do not overlap, such as two Koreatowns in New York, are both kept.
      • Chicago has the City's 77 community areas (from the City's 2020 tract assignments; eight tracts missing from that file, three of them in South Shore, are placed in the community area that holds most of their area) and the City's 98 neighborhoods; a few, such as Magnificent Mile, are much smaller than a tract and carry the same warning. Names in common use that are not official, such as Pilsen, come from Who's On First, and searching one that sits inside a community area, such as Pilsen in Lower West Side, also offers the community area.
      • Fit measures how well the tracts trace a boundary, not whether the boundary itself is right: their overlap as a share of the area and the tracts combined (100% is exact). Each area takes the tracts with at least half their area inside it, or the one tract that covers most of it. The page also says what share of the area the tracts cover and what share of the tracts lies inside it.
      • ZIP codes are Census ZCTAs: the tracts with at least half their land in the ZCTA, or the one tract that covers most of it, with fit measured on land area. They will not match postal ZIPs exactly.
      • Cities and towns are Census places: the tracts with at least half their blocks in the place. A town that makes up less than half of every tract it touches (most towns under a few thousand people, and a few spread-out places) opens the tract that holds the most of it, and the page says the figures cover that whole tract and, when that tract holds under half the town, how much of it.
      • Suggested areas: anyone can suggest a selection of tracts as a named area. It shows in their own search on that device at once, and in everyone's once it is reviewed and approved, labeled as a suggestion. The page does not ask who is suggesting and saves nothing about them.
      • Boundaries are 2024 Census cartographic files (2020 tracts), simplified for the map. Tracts that are only water have no households and are not drawn.
      • Streets on the map come from OpenStreetMap. Pointing at a tract (or tapping it on a phone), zoomed in to street level, lists the named streets that run along its edges, matched within about 45 meters of the simplified tract lines, so now and then a name is missed or a nearby parallel street is listed. Only streets in the part of the map on screen are checked.

      Limits

      • Small areas are noisy. Jobs are reported by workplace block and can be miscoded, and a single large store can swing a category.
      • Self-employed retail sellers and food trucks are not counted (see Local sales above), which overstates leakage a little where those are common.
      • Product-line shares are national averages for each store type; a local store can sell a different mix.
      • Pet spending in the survey includes veterinary care, but vet clinics are not among the store types, so local sales of pets, toys and books lean low.
      • Where a ZIP's figures hide which kind of store holds its jobs (for example, supermarket or convenience store), the jobs are split by the national mix of those store types.
      • Online spending is inside every gap; no neighborhood captures all of it.
      • The ranges show only how far the two supply estimates disagree, and the two share inputs (each uses the ZIP's industry mix and tract job shares). Demand is a single estimate. Real uncertainty is wider than the ranges.
      • Tobacco sales include cannabis where it is sold legally; the survey's tobacco line does not.
      • Totals net categories against each other; read the category rows before siting any one kind of store.
      • The Economic Census is from 2022 and is restated in 2024 dollars, not re-surveyed.
