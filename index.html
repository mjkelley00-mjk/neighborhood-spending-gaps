Neighborhood Spending Gaps

Pick any neighborhood or group of census tracts in the US. See what residents spend in fourteen categories, what local stores sell in twelve of them, and how that compares with the national pattern.

How to read this page

What it measures

For any group of census tracts, we estimate what residents spend in each category, using national spending patterns for households at their incomes, and what stores located there sell. Both come from public data: Census and BLS surveys of households, and Census counts of stores and jobs. It is not card data, so it shows where spending likely goes, not where residents actually shop.

How to read it

The retention rate is local store sales divided by what the national pattern predicts from residents' spending. 1.0 is typical; 0.5 means stores keep half of that, so residents likely shop elsewhere; above 1 means the area likely draws shoppers in. Figures are ranges because store sales are estimated two ways, and real uncertainty is wider. Projected leakage is the dollars of residents' spending the area would keep at the national norm but doesn't. It points to the kinds of businesses the area may be missing. Treat it as a ceiling, not a sales forecast. Full method and limits

Spending and sales

Annual, 2024 dollars. Projected leakage is resident demand × (1 − retention rate): residents' spending the area would keep at the national norm but does not. Negative means the area draws in more than the norm.

What the sales gap could support

Store counts use the sales gap, not the projected leakage above: sales at the national norm minus local sales, because an average store's sales include all its customers, not only residents. Each divides that gap by what an average US benchmark store sells in that category alone (2022 Economic Census, restated in 2024 dollars); a supermarket's grocery sales, for example, leave out its pharmacy and household goods. A gap is a ceiling, not a forecast: some spending will always go online, near work or to destination stores.

Demand-only categories

Public data cannot match these to local sales: most medicine spending is paid through insurance at the pharmacy, and admissions are dominated by visitors. Vehicle purchases are left out entirely as a category no neighborhood captures.

Sales tax on leaked spending

Each category's rate is the combined state and local sales-tax rate for the kinds of things it covers, averaged over the selected tracts by residents' spending: groceries are exempt or taxed at a lower rate in many states, gas is usually taxed by excise instead, and salon services and repair labor are taxed in some states and not others. Projected leakage is in survey dollars, which run below actual spending, so the tax figures lean low. Local restaurant and meals taxes, excise taxes and caps on single purchases are not included.

    Method and limits

    What this measures

    This is a retail-gap estimate, not observed leakage. Local sales include shoppers who live elsewhere, and resident demand is modeled from national spending patterns by income. Neither side records where residents actually shop. That takes card data split by cardholder home and store location, which is the next layer (see the bottom of this page).

    Resident demand

    Residents are each tract's total population and households its occupied housing units (ACS 2020–2024 five-year, tables B01003 and B19001). Residents include people living in group quarters, such as dorms, prisons and nursing homes, who are not households and are not counted in demand; a tract that is mostly group quarters can show many residents and little or no demand. Demand is households by income bracket in each tract (B19001) times what US households at each income spend by category (BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey 2024), interpolated at each bracket's midpoint. The top bracket's mean is solved from aggregate household income (B19025), held between $200K and $1M. Gasoline uses 2022 survey spending at 2022 incomes, restated at 2024 pump prices. Gas, auto repair and vehicle purchases are scaled by the tract's vehicles per household (B25046) against what the survey shows at the same incomes, between 0.2 and 2.5 times.

    Categories

    Twelve categories are measured on both sides: groceries; full-service restaurants; quick-service restaurants, coffee and food trucks; alcohol, from stores and bars; tobacco and vaping; household supplies, garden and stationery; pets, toys, hobbies and books; personal care products and services; apparel, shoes and clothing care; furniture, home furnishings, appliances and computers; gasoline; and auto parts and repair. Medicines and entertainment admissions are demand only.

    Stores sell across categories, so local sales are counted by what is sold, not by store type. Each store type's sales are split by what that kind of store sells nationally, from the 2022 Economic Census product lines: a supermarket's sales go mostly to groceries, partly to household supplies, personal care and alcohol, and its pharmacy counter to no category; a gas station's go to gasoline, groceries, tobacco and alcohol. Medicines and vitamins, prescription or over the counter, fall in no category, matching the demand-only medicines line; so do lines no category covers, such as sporting goods and used goods.

    Full-service and quick-service restaurants split cleanly on the sales side. On the spending side, each tract's restaurant spending is split by the share that households at each income spend at full-service restaurants, from the BLS Consumer Expenditure Diary Survey 2024 public-use files: about 40%–45% for households under $100,000, 46%–48% from $100,000 to $200,000 and 52% above $200,000 (46% for all households; each income range's share has a standard error of 2–5 points). Food from vending machines and mobile vendors counts as quick-service. Employer and school cafeterias and catered events are left out when the share is measured, but their spending stays in restaurant demand and is split in the same proportion.

    Local sales: two estimates

    • Jobs-based (LEHD LODES 2023): retail, food-service and personal-service jobs on each block, split into store types by the ZIP's industry mix and aligned with Economic Census employment for the state.
    • ZIP-based (ZIP Business Patterns 2023): each ZIP's employment by store type, with each Census-suppressed size class given the fewest stores the suppression rule allows, each store counted at its size class's midpoint, and the remainder scaled to the ZIP's published sector total, then allocated to tracts by their share of the ZIP's jobs in that sector.
    • Both estimates add self-employed businesses (Census Nonemployer Statistics 2024) for restaurants, bars, salons, barbers and other personal care, auto repair, coin laundries and dry cleaners: each county's receipts, placed in tracts by their share of the county's jobs in that industry. Self-employed retail sellers are left out, because most of their sales are online or direct, and so are food trucks, which the Census reports together with caterers.

    Each store type's jobs are multiplied by its 2022 Economic Census sales per employee, taken from the place, county, metro area, state or US, whichever is the smallest with enough stores (pharmacies always at the US rate), split into categories as above, and restated in 2024 dollars (gasoline at 2024 pump prices). Thirty store types are used, from supermarkets and convenience stores to salons, dry cleaners and food trucks; building-material stores, car dealers and online sellers are left out. The two estimates set every range on this page. Alaska uses LODES 2016 and Michigan 2021, the latest each state has published.

    Retention rate

    Across the US, store sales run 0.9 to 3.5 times modeled resident spending, depending on the category. Most run above it, because households under-report spending in the survey and stores also sell to businesses, commuters and visitors. Pets, toys and books and home furnishings run near or below it, because much of that spending goes online or to store types left out here. The retention rate divides local sales by resident demand times that national ratio, separately for each estimate, so 1.0 means an area sells what the national pattern predicts for its residents. Projected leakage is resident demand times (1 − retention rate): residents' spending, in survey dollars, that local stores would keep at the national norm but do not. Because the survey under-reports some spending, it leans conservative. Store counts instead use the gap in store-sales terms (the predicted sales minus local sales), divided by a benchmark store's sales in that category.

    Check against the South Shore baseline

    For Chicago's South Shore (the City's 15 tracts), this page finds 25,043 households (CMAP's figure is 26,470) and $330M of resident demand in the five categories the hand-built South Shore baseline measured (the baseline has $352M from the same survey items; the difference comes from the household count, the baseline's assumption for top-bracket income, and its single car-ownership adjustment for the whole area where this page adjusts tract by tract). The baseline compared local sales with demand directly, found local sales at 63%–75% of demand and a gap of $88M–$129M. This page measures 12 categories against the national norm and splits store sales by what each kind of store sells, so its figures are larger and not directly comparable: a net projected leakage of $232M–$263M, with retention rates of 0.50–0.57 for groceries, 0.05–0.12 for full-service dining, 0.22–0.45 for quick-service food and 0.12–0.14 for home furnishings. The two agree on where the biggest gaps are: restaurants and everyday goods (here groceries, household supplies, and pets, toys and books). Home furnishings, which the baseline could not measure, shows almost no local sales.

    Check against states' sales-tax records

    Many states publish taxable sales by city or county and by kind of business. Where they do, the estimates here are compared with them.

    Restaurants and bars are the cleanest comparison, since every state taxes restaurant meals. Store goods compare less directly: taxable sales leave out groceries in most states and include goods outside the twelve categories here (electronics, sporting goods, jewelry and more), so the estimates usually come to less than taxable sales there, and the correlation is the test. The largest misses are small towns where tract lines cut through the commercial district, warehouse towns, and places whose sales come mostly from hotels and conventions, like Rosemont, Illinois.

    Jobs, stores and programs

    • Jobs located here come from the same LEHD LODES files, by workplace and all 20 industry sectors. Jobs held by residents come from LODES residence files, wherever those jobs are. Both count jobs, not people, so their difference is net commuting. Self-employed people are not in either.
    • Stores here: food stores are USDA's list of every store authorized to take SNAP (downloaded October 2026). Other kinds are places listed in Overture Maps (October 2026 release, places with a confidence score of at least 0.9 and not marked closed), an open dataset compiled from several map sources; it lists more salons, bars and home furnishings stores than Census business counts, so treat those rows as indications. National rates per 10,000 residents use the same sources.
    • Sales tax on leaked spending applies each state's combined state and local sales-tax rates to projected leakage, category by category: the rate where the selected tracts are (the city's when at least half a tract is in it, otherwise the town's or the county's), and only on what each state taxes. Groceries, gas, salon services and repair labor are exempt or taxed differently in many states; personal care, apparel and auto are split between goods and services by the services' national share of sales. Rates come from each state's revenue department (or the Streamlined Sales Tax files its members publish), as of October 2026. Local restaurant taxes are included where a state publishes them (Massachusetts, Mississippi, Montana, and meals taxes in DC, Connecticut, Maine, New Hampshire, Rhode Island and Vermont); other local meals taxes, excise taxes and caps on single purchases are not. Gaps for each state are listed with the figures and in the table below.
    • Programs and designations come from the agencies' tract lists: Treasury's 2018 Opportunity Zones and its September 2026 list of tracts eligible for 2027 zones; the CDFI Fund's New Markets Tax Credit and CDFI Investment Area eligibility (2016–2020 ACS); and USDA's food access maps (2019 supermarket access; 2025 access to any SNAP store). Lists drawn on 2010 tracts are carried to a 2020 tract when at least half its land lies in listed 2010 tracts. "Severely distressed" here applies the three main NMTC tests only; a tract can also qualify in other ways.

    Neighborhoods and other areas

    • Neighborhoods outside Chicago come from Who's On First, an open gazetteer. In some cities its boundaries come from the city's own planning or GIS office (Atlanta, Baltimore, Boston, Denver, Los Angeles, New Orleans, Oakland, Portland, San Diego, San Francisco, Seattle and others) or from published maps (Philadelphia, Washington, New York); elsewhere most come from Zetashapes and Quattroshapes, algorithmic boundaries assembled from crowd-sourced place data. Each result names its source. There is no official national neighborhood map, so coverage and quality are uneven. Algorithmic boundaries (Zetashapes, Quattroshapes) that tracts trace poorly (fit under 30%) are left out as noise. Boundaries from every other source are kept at any fit, with a warning when the tracts trace them poorly. Where one city has two overlapping boundaries with the same name, the City's or a city office's own boundary is kept, then the one the tracts trace better; same-name places that do not overlap, such as two Koreatowns in New York, are both kept.
    • Chicago has the City's 77 community areas (from the City's 2020 tract assignments; eight tracts missing from that file, three of them in South Shore, are placed in the community area that holds most of their area) and the City's 98 neighborhoods; a few, such as Magnificent Mile, are much smaller than a tract and carry the same warning. Names in common use that are not official, such as Pilsen, come from Who's On First, and searching one that sits inside a community area, such as Pilsen in Lower West Side, also offers the community area.
    • Fit measures how well the tracts trace a boundary, not whether the boundary itself is right: their overlap as a share of the area and the tracts combined (100% is exact). Each area takes the tracts with at least half their area inside it, or the one tract that covers most of it. The page also says what share of the area the tracts cover and what share of the tracts lies inside it.
    • ZIP codes are Census ZCTAs: the tracts with at least half their land in the ZCTA, or the one tract that covers most of it, with fit measured on land area. They will not match postal ZIPs exactly.
    • Cities and towns are Census places: the tracts with at least half their blocks in the place.
    • Suggested areas: anyone can suggest a selection of tracts as a named area. It shows in their own search on that device at once, and in everyone's once it is reviewed and approved, labeled as a suggestion. The page does not ask who is suggesting and saves nothing about them.
    • Boundaries are 2024 Census cartographic files (2020 tracts), simplified for the map. Tracts that are only water have no households and are not drawn.
    • Streets on the map come from OpenStreetMap. Pointing at a tract (or tapping it on a phone), zoomed in to street level, lists the named streets that run along its edges, matched within about 45 meters of the simplified tract lines, so now and then a name is missed or a nearby parallel street is listed. Only streets in the part of the map on screen are checked.

    Limits

    • Small areas are noisy. Jobs are reported by workplace block and can be miscoded, and a single large store can swing a category.
    • Self-employed retail sellers and food trucks are not counted (see Local sales above), which overstates leakage a little where those are common.
    • Product-line shares are national averages for each store type; a local store can sell a different mix.
    • Pet spending in the survey includes veterinary care, but vet clinics are not among the store types, so local sales of pets, toys and books lean low.
    • Where a ZIP's figures hide which kind of store holds its jobs (for example, supermarket or convenience store), the jobs are split by the national mix of those store types.
    • Online spending is inside every gap; no neighborhood captures all of it.
    • The ranges show only how far the two supply estimates disagree, and the two share inputs (each uses the ZIP's industry mix and tract job shares). Demand is a single estimate. Real uncertainty is wider than the ranges.
    • Tobacco sales include cannabis where it is sold legally; the survey's tobacco line does not.
    • Totals net categories against each other; read the category rows before siting any one kind of store.
    • The Economic Census is from 2022 and is restated in 2024 dollars, not re-surveyed.
